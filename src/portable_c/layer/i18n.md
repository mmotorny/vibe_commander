# Language, text and time: a survey for the layer

**Draft, all of it. Nothing here is decided; what binds it is under
"Decided" of `LAYER.md`. Made on 13 October 2026, for step 3 of the road
(`CLAUDE.md`, "The portable C").**

This is one area of the layer's design, made from the platforms' side as
the user asked: for each thing, what DOS, POSIX, Windows and Apple's
systems provide, where they agree, where one is of another shape, and
then what common ground there is and how the program's needs fall on it.
The program's needs are those of `LAYER.md`, "What the program needs",
and its section "What UTF-8 inside touches".

## How this was made

Nothing was tried on any platform but DOS, and on DOS only by reading
the goldens that the bench has recorded. Each claim below carries a mark:

- **R**: read in the documentation that is linked, during this survey.
- **D**: what the linked documentation says, as known before this
  survey and not read again for it. Worth a look before it is relied on.
- **I**: inferred: from the documentation, from how the platforms are
  known to work, or from the program. Not documented as such.
- **B**: seen: in the bench's goldens, in Open Watcom's headers in the
  build, or in the locale data of the development machine (glibc 2.43,
  Ubuntu), read with `locale` and in its source files.

Ralf Brown's list could not be reached; DOS's calls are from HelpPC
2.10, a reference of 1991 for the same calls.

## The differences that matter

1. **Who knows the user's conventions.** On Windows and Apple they are
   the user's settings, and only the system's own calls see them. On
   POSIX they are the environment, which a program of the GUI on macOS
   and iOS does not get, and which names a locale that may not be
   installed. On DOS they are one record, three orders and four bytes.
2. **Patterns against facts.** Every platform but DOS describes a date
   by a pattern, which may name months, put text between fields, or have
   a separator of several bytes. DOS has an order and one separator. The
   program writes a date in 8 cells and a time in 6. A pattern can be
   read down to DOS's facts for the countries of Europe and the Americas
   and ISO's `y-M-d` (I); not without loss for Chinese, Japanese or
   Korean, whose patterns have a word between fields (I).
3. **Separators are not one byte.** U+202F in glibc's ru_RU (B), a
   no-break space of some kind elsewhere (I). Each is one character and
   one cell, in UTF-8 two or three bytes.
4. **Order of names.** The three file managers sort digits as numbers
   and ignore case, each by its own rules, and two of the three change
   their order between releases. DOS sorts by bytes. Nothing gives the
   same order on every platform but an order that the program makes
   itself.
5. **Capitals.** One character's capital, Unicode's simple mapping, is
   what all can do, but POSIX only in a UTF-8 locale and Windows only
   below U+10000 per call. A text's capitals in full, `ß` to `SS`, the
   Turkish `i`, change the length and depend on the language: only
   Windows and Apple have them, and ICU. DOS has one character to one
   byte, by the code page, and the user's decision that a search asks
   DOS for every byte.
6. **Width.** Only POSIX has a call, and in a terminal the terminal
   decides, by its own table. `☁️` is U+2601 and U+FE0F, the selector
   that asks for an emoji: a terminal that honours it gives two cells,
   one that does not gives one (I). On iOS the program draws the grid and
   so decides itself.
7. **Normalization.** The same name may come in two forms of Unicode on
   Apple's file systems, which treat them as one; Linux treats them as
   two. A search and a comparison have to say which they mean.
8. **The program's texts.** No platform but DOS lacks a place for
   translations, and each has its own. The scenarios hold the English
   screens of the original.
9. **Time.** DOS has local time and no zone, and no clock that does not
   go back. Every other has UTC, a zone and a monotonic clock. Open
   Watcom's `time_t` cannot be the layer's.
10. **Who runs the loop.** On iOS the system calls the program; the
    program cannot wait. The blanker and the zooming boxes wait for the
    next tick in a loop today.

## Candidate shapes

All three have the same for time (below, "Time in every shape"); they
differ in conventions and text.

### A. Facts: the layer tells, the program writes

The layer answers with a record of the program's needs in the layer's
values: the order of the date (three), its separator, 12 or 24 hours,
the thousands' and the decimal separator, each a character as UTF-8. The
program formats and parses dates, times and numbers itself, in its fixed
columns, as `format.c` and `dirfunc.c` do now. Text is the program's
too: capitals by a table of Unicode's simple mapping, names in order by
code point after capitals, a width table.

| Platform | Cost |
|---|---|
| DOS | little to build: 38h fills the record. But the program's own order and capitals are not DOS's bytes: Ё and the frames sort elsewhere than in CP866, and a search no longer asks DOS for each byte. **Breaks the user's decision on capitals, and screens of Russian names** |
| POSIX | read `D_FMT`, `T_FMT`, `RADIXCHAR`, `THOUSEP` and reduce the patterns (I: some 50 lines) |
| Windows | `GetLocaleInfoEx` of four values, reduced likewise |
| Apple | `CFLocale` and a template's pattern, reduced likewise |

What the program changes: `md.Country` read through the record; tables
of Unicode in the common code. What it loses: the platform's order of
names, its formats where they do not reduce.

### B. Services: the platform formats, compares and maps

The layer formats a date, a time and a number to UTF-8, compares two
names, maps a text to capitals, says a character's width, each the
platform's own way. The program lays out what comes back.

| Platform | Cost |
|---|---|
| DOS | the DOS implementation holds what `format.c` does now, under the layer, with 38h's record its own; every format and parse moves out of the program |
| POSIX | `strftime`, `strcoll`, `towupper`, `wcwidth`, each tied to `setlocale` of the process, or `newlocale` and `_l` (D) |
| Windows | `GetDateFormatEx`, `CompareStringEx`, `LCMapStringEx`, all UTF-16: a conversion each way at each call |
| Apple | `CFDateFormatter`, `CFStringCompare`, `CFStringUppercase`: a `CFString` made of each text at each call |

What the program changes: every column that shows a date or a time takes
text of varying width, `Sep 16, 2026` or `16.09.2026` against `9-16-26`.
The panels' layout is then the platform's, and the Attributes dialog's
date field needs a parse of the platform's, which Windows has only in
OLE (D). The original's screens stay only on DOS.

### C. One engine: ICU in the common code

The layer gives a locale's name, BCP 47, and the time; ICU does the
rest in common code: formats, collation, capitals, normalization.

| Platform | Cost |
|---|---|
| DOS | none can be had: ICU is megabytes of code and data, of C++ for its build, and not for Open Watcom (I). DOS keeps another path entirely |
| POSIX | the distribution's ICU, with its version in the library's name, or a pinned build of our own of tens of MB (I) |
| Windows | in the system since 10 1703, but its data "may not align" with the system's own (R) |
| Apple | not public: our own build in the app (I) |

What the program changes: little, but two programs in effect, one with
ICU and DOS without.

### Recommended: A for conventions, B for text

The record of facts for dates, times and numbers, since the program's
columns are fixed and DOS's record is exactly that; and services for
text, since what DOS does there, by the user's decision, is ask DOS
character by character, which only a service can keep, and what the
other platforms do best, an order of names like their file managers',
is theirs alone. Every value is of standard C: `char` holding UTF-8,
`int64_t`, `struct tm`, an `enum`; none is DOS's. The names are
placeholders.

- **Conventions: a record, `struct Conventions`.** Date order (an enum
  of three: month-day-year, day-month-year, year-month-day), date
  separator, time separator, 24 hours or 12, thousands' separator,
  decimal separator: each separator one character, as a UTF-8 text of
  at most 4 bytes and a zero. The program formats as now. DOS: 38h, at
  the same place, with the 128 capitals asked behind it as now. Others:
  read from the user's settings, Windows `GetLocaleInfoEx`, Apple
  `CFLocale` and a template's pattern, Linux `nl_langinfo` after
  `setlocale(LC_ALL, "")`, reduced to the record; what does not reduce
  takes the first separator and the order of the fields (I).
- **Text: services on UTF-8.** `TextUpper(c)`: one character's capital,
  simple mapping; on DOS the code page's byte asked of DOS, every time,
  as decided; elsewhere one table of Unicode's simple mapping shared by
  the three implementations (see the questions: the platforms' calls
  differ by locale, cost and width of `wchar_t`). `TextLower(c)` the
  same by the table that DOS's answers make. `NameOrder(a, b)`: the
  file manager's order; on DOS the bytes in the code page, as `CmpPar`
  compares them; Windows `CompareStringEx` with `SORT_DIGITSASNUMBERS`
  and `LINGUISTIC_IGNORECASE`, or `StrCmpLogicalW`; Apple
  `CFStringCompareWithOptionsAndLocale` as Finder's; Linux `strcoll`
  (or GLib's key, a dependency: a question). `TextWidth(c)`: cells, 1 on
  DOS, `wcwidth` in a UTF-8 locale on Linux, a table elsewhere.
  `TextFromSystem` and `TextToSystem`, on DOS by the code page's 256
  glyphs (frames and arrows of 437 and 866 included, both ways; which
  code page, question 4), a copy elsewhere, UTF-16 on Windows.
- **The program's texts** stay the program's tables, English, now in
  UTF-8; the layer gives nothing for language until translations are
  wanted.
- **Time in every shape.** `TimeLocal()`: a `struct tm` of standard C
  and the milliseconds, local; DOS 2Ch, hundredths times ten. `Clock()`:
  milliseconds as an `int64_t` from an origin that does not matter, for
  differences; DOS the ticks of 40:6Ch times 54.925 (I; midnight is
  question 8). File dates: an `int64_t` of seconds since 1970 and the
  nanoseconds, UTC where the system has UTC; on DOS the local time
  counted as if it were UTC, so that turning it into a `struct tm` is
  arithmetic and asks DOS nothing. `TimeLocalOf(instant)` and its
  inverse for the Attributes dialog, with `tm_isdst` -1. `Wait(until)`:
  on DOS INT 28h once and back, as `Idle` is now; elsewhere a wait for
  input or the time; on iOS the program's loop has to become called (an
  area of its own, the events).

What DOS does under it: the same calls in the same order. 38h once at
the start, then `CaseMap` for 80h to FFh, then a call of DOS's routine
for every byte that `UpCase` is asked for, 2Ch where `TimeOfDay` is
called, 40:6Ch where `Ticks` is read, INT 28h where `Idle` is called. The
screens stay, since the record's values are DOS's and the program
formats as it does, the order of names is that of the bytes, and the
code page's glyphs map both ways one to one.

- A wait for the next tick becomes a wait of a period. With `Clock` in
  milliseconds made of ticks on DOS, a period of 54 ms ends at the next
  tick, as the wait does now; and "under 3 ticks" is under any figure
  from 110 to 164 ms, 2 and 3 ticks being 109.9 and 164.8 ms. Each such
  constant is chosen so that on DOS it gives the ticks it gives now (I).
- The order of names by extension, by time and by size stays the
  program's; `NameOrder` is only for two names.

## Questions for the user

1. **Dates, times, numbers: who writes them?** (a) The layer reports the
   country's facts, order of the date, separators, 12 or 24 hours; the
   program writes them in its fixed columns, as today. (b) The
   platform formats them, natively, in widths that vary. Recommended:
   (a). The panels keep their columns, DOS is unchanged.
2. **The order of names in a panel.** (a) As the platform's file
   manager: Finder, Explorer, digits as numbers. (b) The platform's
   plain collation. (c) Code points after capitals, the same
   everywhere. DOS keeps its byte order in each. Recommended: (a); its
   order may change with the system's version.
3. **Capitals beyond ASCII, off DOS.** (a) One table of Unicode's
   simple case mapping, made from Unicode's data, pinned and fetched at
   build. (b) Each platform's own call. DOS asks DOS per byte, as
   decided. Recommended: (a): the same answer everywhere, no locale
   surprises. It is a new download.
4. **The code page on DOS.** UTF-8 inside needs it, for names and the
   screen. (a) Ask DOS once at the start, 6601h: a call the original
   does not make, declared. (b) Guess it from the country that 38h
   returns already. (c) A setting. Recommended: (a); (b) is wrong for
   850.
5. **The program's language.** (a) English only for now; the layer
   gives nothing for language. (b) Prepare translations now: the layer
   gives the user's languages, texts move to catalogs. Recommended: (a):
   the scenarios hold English screens; ask again after Linux.
6. **Where conventions come from on Linux and macOS.** (a) Linux from
   the environment, `LANG` and `LC_*`, as every terminal program; macOS
   from the user's settings, as iOS. (b) Both from the environment.
   Recommended: (a): on macOS the environment misses the user's own
   changes, and is empty outside Terminal.
7. **A character's width.** `☁️` may take two cells. (a) The layer
   answers: 1 on DOS, `wcwidth` on Linux, a table of Unicode's on
   Windows and Apple. (b) The program's own table everywhere.
   Recommended: (a): the width has to be what the terminal draws.
8. **The ticks at midnight.** The BIOS's count starts again at
   midnight; the original then misses a tap on Alt, little more. (a)
   DOS's clock starts again too, as the original's. (b) It counts on,
   asking DOS nothing: a fix, declared. Elsewhere the clock never goes
   back. Recommended: (a) until a fix is wanted.
9. **The setup's Country info off DOS.** It names `config.sys` and
   shows the code page's upper half. (a) Keep the box with the
   platform's conventions, without the table and that line. (b) Remove
   it there, as a thing of DOS. Recommended: (a).
10. **A Unicode library.** (a) None: platform calls and small tables of
    Unicode's data. (b) ICU: the system's on Linux and Windows, our own
    build on Apple, none on DOS. Recommended: (a): DOS needs another
    path anyway, and ICU on Apple is megabytes of our own.

## What is not sure

- That the patterns of the platforms reduce to DOS's facts for the
  countries that matter: inferred, not tried. Which countries' short
  dates do not reduce, a list is to be made.
- How Linux file managers sort: three of them, each its own way; the
  recommendation takes `strcoll`, which is `ls`'s, for want of one.
- Whether Windows Terminal and conhost give `☁️` one cell or two, and
  what Terminal on macOS does: not read.
- Whether `6601h` is answered by FreeDOS on the bench as by MS-DOS: not
  tried; nor whether a guess from the country (question 4, b) would hold
  for the two benches, which it would for 1 and 7 (I).
- The blanker starts by the time of day, as the original: a clock set
  back, or the end of summer time, puts it off by up to an hour. Off
  DOS it could go by `Clock` instead; that would be a deviation, not
  asked here.
- That a tap on Alt is all that the ticks' midnight changes (question
  8): read in `input.c`, `blank.c` and `box.c`, not tried.
- Names in small letters: the original shows FAT's capitals as small
  letters, `LetLin`. Where names keep their case, whether to go on doing
  so is a question for the panels, not of this area.
- Whether `md.Country` reaches the setup file: not looked at; if it
  does, the record has to be kept there in DOS's layout.
- The cost of `NameOrder` on Apple: a `CFString` of each name, made once
  per name and kept for the sort (I); a sort on Windows by keys,
  `LCMAP_SORTKEY`, may be cheaper (I).
- Android is not in the list. Its C library knows only the locales "C"
  and "C.UTF-8", and its documentation sends programs to ICU or Java
  (R, [bionic][an-bionic]): it would want shape C or Java, and changes
  the picture if it is ever a target.
